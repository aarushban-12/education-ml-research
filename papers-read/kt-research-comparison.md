### Comparison of Existing Knowledge Tracing Research and Ability-Stratified Reliability

Recent research has increasingly focused on improving knowledge tracing models for predicting student performance. Models such as Bayesian Knowledge Tracing (BKT), logistic-regression-based approaches, and more recent attention-based models such as Self-Attentive Knowledge Tracing (SAKT) use different methods to model students' knowledge and predict whether they will answer future questions correctly. Their primary objective is generally to improve predictive performance, with metrics such as AUC, accuracy, and log loss commonly used to evaluate how well models distinguish between correct and incorrect future responses.

After reviewing these papers, I found that knowledge tracing has become a well-developed research direction, with substantial work comparing different architectures and improving next-question prediction. However, much of this evaluation focuses on aggregate predictive performance across the entire student population. While some research has examined fairness and differences between learner groups, there is less emphasis on whether the probabilities produced by knowledge tracing models are equally reliable across students with different levels of prior ability.

My research addresses this evaluation question. Instead of proposing another knowledge tracing architecture, I will reproduce established models, including Logistic Regression, BKT, and SAKT, and evaluate the reliability of their next-question predictions across student ability groups. Students will be stratified into ability quartiles using information available before the evaluation period, and calibration and prediction reliability will be evaluated separately for each group. This approach aims to determine whether knowledge tracing predictions are equally trustworthy for students with different levels of prior ability, and whether this relationship differs between simpler and more advanced knowledge tracing models.
